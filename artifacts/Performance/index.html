PERFORMANCE EVALUATION¶

What this section does:

We measure how well the model performs overall. But before we look at the numbers, there is an important warning.

Why accuracy is not our metric, the full explanation

Recall our class of 1,000 students with 1 cheater. A teacher who marks every student as not cheating achieves 99.9% accuracy. They catch zero cheaters.

This is the accuracy paradox. Standard accuracy is almost useless when one outcome is extremely rare. We need tools that penalise the model for missing fraud, not just for being wrong in general.

This is the deep reason why aggregate performance metrics must always be paired with per-group analysis. A model can achieve an outstanding MCC while simultaneously providing zero protection to an entire segment of users; because those users contribute so few fraud cases to the overall count that their failures barely register in the aggregate number.

We will demonstrate this precisely in the fairness and bias section.

Metric 1: Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC)

MCC accounts for all four outcomes: fraud correctly caught, legitimate correctly cleared, fraud missed, and legitimate customers wrongly blocked. Scale: negative 1 (perfectly wrong) to 0 (random guessing) to positive 1 (perfect). Our threshold: MCC above 0.50.

Metric 2: Log Loss

Measures both accuracy and confidence. A model that says it is 95% sure something is fraud and is wrong gets penalised far more heavily than one that says 55% sure. In fraud detection, confident wrong predictions are expensive. Our threshold: Log Loss below 0.40.

# ── SECTION 7: PR-AUC AND KS STATISTIC ───────────────────────────────────────
# These measure discrimination power across all possible thresholds,
# not just at the default 0.5 cutoff.

prec_v, rec_v, _ = precision_recall_curve(y_test, y_prob)
pr_auc = auc(rec_v, prec_v)

fraud_scores = y_prob[y_test.values == 1]
legit_scores  = y_prob[y_test.values == 0]
ks_stat, _    = ks_2samp(fraud_scores, legit_scores)

sorted_scores = np.sort(y_prob)
sample_idx    = np.linspace(0, len(sorted_scores) - 1, 5000, dtype=int)
s_sample      = sorted_scores[sample_idx]
f_cdf = np.array([np.mean(fraud_scores <= s) for s in s_sample])
l_cdf = np.array([np.mean(legit_scores  <= s) for s in s_sample])
ks_score = s_sample[np.argmax(np.abs(f_cdf - l_cdf))]

print('=' * 60)
print('DISCRIMINATION POWER — PR-AUC AND KS STATISTIC')
print('=' * 60)
print(f'\nPR-AUC  = {pr_auc:.4f} | Threshold > {THRESHOLDS["PR_AUC"]} '

Model Performance Evaluation — MCC Analysis¶

Three of four performance metrics pass. Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) fails.

The reason is visible in the confusion matrix. The model catches 1,631 out of 1,643 actual fraud cases and has a recall rate of 99.27%, which is exceptional. However, it also wrongly blocks 6,617 legitimate customers. Of everything the model flags as fraud, only 19.77% is actually fraud.

MCC accounts for both sides of that tradeoff and penalises the high false positive count.

This is not a catastrophic finding. High recall with lower precision is a deliberate tradeoff in fraud detection, most operators would rather over-flag and review than miss real fraud. However, it does indicate that the model's decision threshold requires calibration before deployment. The precision issue will be addressed in the Deployment Readiness section.

Structural Performance Insight¶

This gradient confirms the narrative established in both the proxy detection and E.D.A sections:

The more fraud examples a group contributes during training, the better the model performs for that group.

  • High-Balance: 5,738 training examples → MCC 0.62
  • Low-Balance: 35 training examples → MCC 0.12

The relationship is direct, and the consequence is visible even before formal fairness metrics are applied.

Interpretation¶

This is the accuracy paradox made concrete:

  • Aggregate metrics appear strong
  • MCC exposes the precision problem
  • Per-group MCC reveals structural underperformance

Even MCC understates the severity for Low-Balance users, because 6 test cases is too small a sample to fully capture failure behavior. A dedicated fairness analysis is required to properly quantify the risk.
